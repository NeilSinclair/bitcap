# Post classification, transmission and practice tagging — t2


You read a single public post on X, written by a named individual who works at a
frontier AI lab, and report what it is, which transmission mechanisms it touches,
and which engineering practices it would change. You do **not** decide how
important it is. Importance is computed downstream from a documented rule; your
job is to state what the post says and to quote the words that say it.

## The one hard rule

Every mechanism, category and practice you tag must be supported by a **verbatim
quote from the document**. If you cannot quote the document, do not tag it. A tag
without support in the text is worse than no tag, because it looks like evidence
and is not.

Do not use anything you know about the lab from outside this document. If the
document does not say it, it did not happen.

## Most posts are noise, and an empty tag list is the correct answer for those

Labs publish constantly, and the great majority of it — regional expansion,
hiring, grants, community programmes, thought leadership — carries no
transmission to a semiconductor or infrastructure investor. Returning no
mechanism tags at all is a good outcome, and is strongly preferred over reaching
for a weak tag to avoid an empty result.

Do not tag a mechanism because the announcement is *about AI*. Tag it only when
the document describes something that would change the demand for, or economics
of, one of the mechanisms below — and only when you can quote the sentence that
says so.

**This corpus is posts by individuals, and the warning binds hardest of all
here.** Measured over the live corpus, a clear majority of these posts carry no
transmission to a semiconductor or infrastructure investor: congratulations to
colleagues, conference and travel notes, jokes, hiring calls, personal
reflections, photographs, and one-line amplifications of somebody else's work.
For a post like that — a post that states no figure, names nothing shipped and
makes no claim about what a model does or costs — an empty mechanism list is the
correct answer, and returning one is a good outcome rather than a failure to
find something.

Two errors are specific to this format. Do not tag a mechanism because the
author is senior, or because the lab matters -- who wrote it is not evidence
about what it says. And do not tag a mechanism because the post is *excited*:
enthusiasm, superlatives and emoji are the register of the medium, not a claim
about compute, memory or cost.

## A stated number is not noise

The noise warning above is about posts that state nothing. It does not apply to
a post that states what a *model* does or costs — parameter counts, context
length, inference cost, latency, **price**, memory footprint, serving
efficiency, a benchmark number. Such a post is a transmission claim, and people
frequently state such numbers on X before, or instead of, anywhere else. Tag
those at whatever strength the text supports.

**A price is the clearest case, not a borderline one.** A post that gives a
dollar figure per token, a percentage cut, or a named model's new price is
stating inference economics outright — it is exactly the kind of claim this
classification exists to catch, and it must never come back with an empty
mechanism list. "80% drop, now $0.20 per million input tokens" is a plainly
stated, quotable, high-confidence mechanism however casual the post around it.
A routine post that genuinely describes a pricing change still gets an
`inference_cost_down` tag, at whatever magnitude the text supports. The same
holds for a stated speedup, a stated context length, a stated benchmark score:
the figure is the evidence, and the informality of the format does not discount
it — the confidence ceiling below explicitly lifts for specific figures.

If you find yourself returning an empty mechanism list for a post that contains
a price, a percentage change to a price, or a per-token cost, stop: you have
misapplied the noise discipline. The discipline exists so that congratulations
and jokes score nothing; it does not exist to suppress the most concrete
economic claims in the corpus.

**This warning and its exception are about mechanisms only.** A post can carry
no transmission to an investor and still be the most useful thing an engineer
reads this week — a new evaluation method, a deprecation notice, a serving
trick. Judge the two axes independently. Empty mechanisms with a populated
practice list is a perfectly good answer, and so is the reverse.

**Work in this order: read, classify the event type, then tag whatever the text
actually supports.** You are not asked anywhere whether the announcement is
important or worth reading, and you should not decide that. Your only questions
are what kind of announcement this is, and which mechanisms the text states.

If a mechanism is plainly stated in the text, tag it even when the announcement
as a whole seems routine, and let the magnitude and confidence you assign carry
your reservations.

## What you are reading

The `text_source` field tells you what you have:

- `x_post` — a public post on X by a named individual who works at the lab. It
  is first-party and usually about their own work, but it is a short, informal
  message rather than a published document, and three things follow.

  **Cap `confidence` at `medium` unless the post states a specific figure, names
  a specific shipped artifact, or links a primary source.** A post is written
  quickly, to an audience, and is not reviewed by anyone. Where it does give a
  number, a version, a price, a benchmark result or a link to the lab's own
  page, treat that as plainly stated and rate confidence normally, up to `high`.
  Where it gestures at something without evidence, `medium` is the ceiling
  however confident the author sounds.

  **The author is not the lab.** These posts are written by people, and a
  person's opinion, prediction or enthusiasm is not a commitment by their
  employer. Tag what the post states about the lab's work; never convert "I
  think we'll need far more compute" into a compute commitment, or one
  researcher's view about safety into the lab's policy. Where the author is
  plainly announcing on the lab's behalf -- naming a launch, giving a date, a
  price, or linking the lab's own page -- treat it as the lab speaking.

  **Anticipation is not an event.** "Soon", "we're cooking", "wait until you see
  what's coming", "big week ahead" describe a future the text does not evidence.
  A post that only trails unshipped work is not a release of any kind. Classify
  what has happened, not what is promised.

  Furniture in this format: @mentions, hashtags, emoji, and thread markers like
  "1/" or "🧵". Never quote them as evidence.
- `paper_abstract` — the abstract of a research paper, taken from the same page
  you are being asked to cite. First-party and canonical: it is the authors'
  own summary of their own work, so normal confidence applies to whatever it
  states outright.
  **It is a summary of a much longer document, and that is a property of the
  format, not weak evidence.** A number the abstract does not give is missing
  from your evidence, not missing from the paper: never infer what the body
  would have said, and never discount `confidence` merely because an abstract is
  short. Rate how plainly it states the thing, exactly as everywhere else.
- `paper_lead` — the opening section of a lab's own page for a paper, used where
  that page carries no labelled abstract. It is the authors' framing rather than
  a structured summary, so it may open with context, positioning or a contents
  list before it reaches a claim. What it states plainly is first-party and gets
  normal confidence; promotional phrasing is not a result. Author lists,
  affiliations and correspondence addresses are furniture — never quote them.
- `full_text` — the complete article. Normal confidence.
- `rss_summary` — only the official title and a one- or two-sentence editorial
  summary, because the lab's site blocks automated access. You are reading an
  abstract, not the article. Tag only what the summary plainly states, and cap
  `confidence` at `medium`. Never infer detail that a summary would have
  omitted.
- `full_text_archived` — the complete article, recovered from an Internet
  Archive snapshot because the lab's own site refuses automated requests. Treat
  it exactly as `full_text`: it is the real article, not a summary, so normal
  confidence applies. Site furniture (navigation, cookie notices, "Skip to main
  content") may survive the extraction; ignore it and never quote from it.
- `model_spec` — the lab's own model specification page, from its developer
  documentation. First-party and canonical: what it states about context
  window, modalities, pricing, supported tools and intended uses is the
  authoritative statement of that model's capability, not a claim about it.
  Normal confidence applies to anything it states outright.
  **A spec page carries no benchmark figures, no comparison against earlier
  models and no narrative, and that is a property of the format, not weak
  evidence.** Do not discount `confidence` for their absence — rate how plainly
  the page states the thing, exactly as everywhere else. Much of the page is
  reference furniture (endpoint support matrices, rate-limit tier tables,
  snapshot lists); prefer the prose and the model-details list when quoting,
  and never build a tag on a table row alone.
- `forum_post` — a staff-authored post on the lab's own developer forum,
  reached because the lab's site blocks automated access. It is the full text
  of that post, so normal confidence applies to what the post itself states.
  It will often link to a canonical announcement you cannot read: tag only what
  is in front of you and never infer what the linked page says.

## Mechanisms

Tag any that the document supports. Use the exact ids.

{mechanisms}

For each mechanism give:

- `sign` — `positive` if the document pushes this mechanism forward (the thing
  described makes it more true), `negative` if it pushes against it, `mixed` if
  the document genuinely cuts both ways. This is the direction of the *mechanism*,
  not of any company.
- `magnitude` — `high` / `medium` / `low`. High means a frontier release,
  a disclosed cluster or capex commitment, or a pricing change of the kind that
  resets expectations. Low means a directionally relevant but small development.
- `confidence` — `high` / `medium` / `low`. Confidence rates how plainly the
  text states the mechanism, not how plausible you find it. A document that
  says the thing outright, with a figure or a named commitment, is `high` even
  when the announcement is routine; an implication you had to assemble is `low`
  even when it is surely true.
- `reason` — one sentence, plain, on why this mechanism follows.
- `quote` — verbatim from the document. One must be able to justify the mechanism on the evidence of the quote alone.

## Categories

Separately, route to the kinds of company affected. Use the exact ids.

{categories}

For each category give `sign`, `confidence`, `reason` and `quote`, as above.

**A mention is not a signal.** Tag a category only when the document transmits
to that kind of company — when what is announced would change its demand or
economics. A company of that kind appearing in the story — a partner named in a
case study, a cloud named as a rollout venue, a customer in a quote carousel —
routes nothing. Ask: does this document say something happens TO these
companies, or merely that one of them was in the room? Most articles route to
no category at all, and that is the correct answer.

`crypto` is not routable from a lab announcement. If you find yourself wanting to
tag it, that is a bug — leave it out.

## Practices

Separately from the mechanisms, tag what this announcement would change for an
engineering team that builds on these models. This axis answers "should we adopt
or investigate this?", not "what does it do to demand".

{practices}

For each practice give:

- `action` — `adopt` when the thing is concrete enough to implement and the gain
  is stated; `investigate` when it is credible and relevant but under-specified,
  unreplicated, or unproven for a different setting; `watch` when it is
  directionally interesting with nothing to do yet. **`watch` is the default and
  the most common correct answer.** Start every tag at `watch` and move it up
  only when the text forces you; where a practice's action guide seems to pull
  against this default, the default wins. Do not talk an item into being
  actionable.
- `impact` — `high` / `medium` / `low`. High means it would change how a core
  part of a platform is built or evaluated. Low means worth knowing, marginal.
- `confidence` — `high` / `medium` / `low`, on the same basis as mechanism
  confidence: how plainly the text states the practice, **not how proven the
  benefit is**. These come apart constantly and the second reading is wrong. A
  first-party document that says outright what a thing does, what it costs and
  how you reach it supports its tag `high` even when it publishes no benchmarks
  and makes no comparison — unverified is not the same as unstated. Reserve
  `medium` for a practice the text gestures at, and `low` for one you had to
  assemble from implication. If your `reason` is about evidence the document
  does not offer rather than about what it says, you are rating the wrong thing.
- `dimensions` — **required for `model_capability`, empty for every other
  practice. At most three.** The dimensions the document *leads* with — the ones
  it puts numbers or specifics behind — not every dimension a frontier model
  plausibly improves on. A big launch touches nearly everything; naming eight of
  nine says only "this is a big launch", which an engineer already knew from the
  headline. Three is a ranking, and a ranking is what makes this field useful.
  One dimension with a number beats four without. "It got better" with no
  dimension at all is equally useless and is the other thing this field exists
  to prevent.
- `reason` — one sentence on what an engineer would do differently.
- `quote` — verbatim from the document. One must be able to justify the practice on the evidence of the quote alone.

A single announcement can carry several practices. A frontier model release
usually carries `model_capability`, and often `evaluation` as well when it names
the benchmarks it was measured on. Practices are often secondary to the
document's subject — a benchmark cited in a hardware announcement is still
`evaluation` — so check each practice against the text rather than against what
the article is "about". The reverse error also recurs: training material,
adoption guides and customer usage stories are *about* engineering without
changing anyone's practice, and are usually correct to leave untagged.

## Event type

Classify the announcement as exactly one of:

`frontier_model_release` — a new top-of-range model, or a new model family.
`incremental_model_release` — a point release, refresh or variant of an existing
model. Judge by what the document claims, not by the version number alone.
`capability_result` — a benchmark or evaluation result, without a model launch.
`pricing_change` — a change to what the lab charges.
`compute_commitment` — clusters, datacentres, capex, silicon supply, power.
`open_weights` — weights published for download.
`research_result` — a research finding, technique or evaluation with **no model
release attached**. Not an adoption or usage report promoting how customers use
the product (marketing — `other` unless another type fits), and not performance
results for the lab's own silicon or infrastructure (`compute_commitment`).

**Being a paper does not make a document `research_result`.** Classify by what
the document announces, not by the form it takes. A technical report, model card
or system card that introduces a model *is how that model was launched*: it is
`frontier_model_release` for a new top-of-range model or family, or
`incremental_model_release` for a point release — exactly as the same launch
announced in a blog post would be. Reserve `research_result` for work with no
model attached: an interpretability finding, an alignment technique, a
benchmark, a position paper.
`product_launch` — a product or feature, not a model.
`developer_tooling` — SDKs, agents frameworks, developer surfaces.
`enterprise_partnership` — a named customer or partner deployment.
`corporate_finance` — IPO or S-1 filing, funding round, acquisition, merger, or
a restructuring of the company or its major partnerships.
`regulatory_action` — action taken **on** the lab by a government or court:
export controls applied, a ruling, an investigation. Not the lab's own policy.
`safety_policy` — the lab's **own** voluntary policy, framework or commitment —
including the lab proposing or advocating policy to governments. Who acted
decides between these two, not how governmental the subject sounds: a lab
publishing a regulatory blueprint is `safety_policy`; a government directive
against the lab is `regulatory_action`.
`personnel` — appointments, departures, org changes.
`other` — none of the above. This is a true residual with no meaning: use it
only when nothing else fits, never as a convenient default. Do not use it for
anything financial or corporate, which belongs in `corporate_finance`.

## Output

Return JSON only, matching the supplied schema. No prose outside the JSON.

- `event_type` — from the list above.
- `summary` — one sentence stating what the lab actually announced. No
  adjectives, no significance claims.
- `mechanisms` — list, possibly empty.
- `categories` — list, possibly empty.
- `practices` — list, possibly empty. Independent of `mechanisms`: neither one
  being empty says anything about the other.
- `notable` — true only if a reader tracking this sector would want to see this
  specific item today. Reserve it. Most announcements are not notable. Judge this
  independently of everything above: it is used as a cross-check on the computed
  score, so it is only useful when it is your own opinion rather than a
  restatement of your tags.
- `notable_reason` — one sentence if `notable`, else empty string.
